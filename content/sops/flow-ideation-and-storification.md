## Description

This SOP explains how a DPK team member turns a chosen activity into a working lesson flow, then uses storification to give the lesson a narrative that hooks students and connects each part.

Flow is the sequence and pacing of the lesson. Storification is the story thread that gives the flow meaning. Together they turn a single activity into a complete, engaging class experience.

## When This SOP Is Used

Use this SOP when:

- You have chosen an activity and need to build a full lesson flow
- A lesson feels flat, disjointed, or lacking in energy
- Students lose focus between parts of a lesson
- You need to hook students at the start and hold them through the end
- Activities exist but it is unclear how to order them

## Inputs Needed Before Starting

- The chosen activity from activity ideation
- The learning objective
- The grade, subject, and topic
- The total class time available
- The students and their language level
- The materials and setup for each activity

## Steps

### Step 1: List the Activity Parts

Break the chosen activity into its natural pieces: opening, demonstration, hands-on task, discussion, reflection. Do not assign times yet.

### Step 2: Order by Learning Logic

Arrange the parts so each builds on the last:

1. Hook (grab attention)
2. Connect (link to prior knowledge)
3. Experience (do the activity)
4. Make meaning (discuss and reflect)
5. Apply or close (pull it together)

Ask: at every step, does the student know why they are doing this next?

### Step 3: Assign Time to Each Part

Divide the total class time across the parts. Leave the hands-on core the most time and keep the hook and close short.

Check the total equals your class length. Rework the flow if one part runs too long.

### Step 4: Choose a Story Frame

Pick a simple narrative that holds the lesson together and matches the objective. Story frames include:

- You are a team on a mission
- You are solving a real problem
- You are exploring an unfamiliar place
- You are the inventor or designer
- You are investigating a mystery

The story must serve the learning, not compete with it.

### Step 5: Weave the Story Through the Flow

Give each flow part a story beat instead of a plain instruction:

- Hook becomes the story's opening or challenge
- Activity becomes the story's task
- Discussion becomes the team deciding or reporting
- Close becomes the resolution or reflection

Keep the story simple enough that it does not confuse the instructions.

### Step 6: Check Pacing and Transitions

Review the flow as a student would experience it:

- Does the hook lead smoothly into the content?
- Does the story connect each part?
- Are instructions clear at every transition?
- Is there a natural high point in the middle?
- Does the close bring the lesson to a clear end?

### Step 7: Record the Flow

Write the finished flow with part names, times, story beats, and the transition notes between parts. Identify where a Curiosity Activity and Thinking (CAT) fits best in the flow.

## Flow and Storification Checklist

Before designing the CAT for this flow, check:

- All activity parts are listed
- Parts are ordered by learning logic
- Total time matches the class length
- The chosen story serves the objective
- The hook grabs attention
- Each part has a story beat
- Transitions between parts are smooth
- The close wraps the lesson clearly
- The flow is recorded for handoff

## Outputs

- A timed lesson flow
- A story frame that supports the lesson
- Story beats for each part
- Transition notes between parts
- A record in the work log or tracker

## Common Mistakes

- Adding a story that fights the content
- Spending too much time on the hook and running out of time for learning
- Making the story so complex it confuses the steps
- Ordering parts by convenience instead of learning logic
- Forgetting transitions between parts
- Skipping the close or reflection

## Quality Gate

The flow is ready only when a student would move through the lesson without losing focus, and each part clearly moves them closer to the learning objective.
