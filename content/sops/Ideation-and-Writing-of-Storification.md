# Karkhana Storification SOP

**SOP Name:** Karkhana Storification  
**Unit:** DPK Unit  
**Version:** v0.2  
**Owner:** Unit Curator  
**Last Reviewed:** [6th September]

---

# 1. Purpose

This SOP explains how to turn a Karkhana lesson or activity into a simple, child-centered story that creates curiosity and naturally leads bacchaharu into making, testing, exploring, and thinking.

Storification is not just adding characters or dialogue.

A good storified lesson should make bacchaharu feel:

> **“I want to try this!”**

Not:

> **“I have to study this.”**

The story should create a meaningful reason for the activity.

---

# 2. What is Storification?

**Storification means turning a lesson or activity into a story-driven experience.**

Instead of directly saying:

> “Today we will learn about magnets.”

A story creates a situation:

> छुक्छुके wants to move a metal ring through a maze without touching it.

Now there is:

- A goal
- A reason
- A difficulty
- A question
- A reason to explore

The scientific or mathematical idea should emerge through the activity.

---

# 3. Why Do We Use Storification?

Storification helps bacchaharu:

- Connect ideas with real life.
- Become curious.
- Ask meaningful questions.
- Explore instead of waiting for answers.
- Make, test, compare, and create.
- Move naturally from story to activity.
- Think about what happened after the activity.
- Feel like problem solvers and creators.

---

# 4. Core Karkhana Story Principle

The most important rule is:

> **Characters should want to DO something, not LEARN something.**

Avoid:

> छुक्छुके wants to learn about magnets.

Write:

> छुक्छुके wants to move the metal ring without touching it.

Avoid:

> तारा wants to understand volume.

Write:

> तारा wants to find the hidden size of a stone.

Useful story goals:

- Make
- Find
- Build
- Move
- Fix
- Create
- Compare
- Guess
- Reach
- Test
- Help
- Solve
- Explore

Avoid using learning itself as the character’s goal.

---

# 5. Understand the Lesson Before Writing

Before writing any story, understand the actual lesson first.

Read the complete CAT flow.

Identify:

- What happens in Curiosity?
- What do bacchaharu actually do in the activity?
- What materials do they use?
- What will they observe?
- What should they discover?
- What happens during Thinking?
- What lesson came before?
- What comes after?

## Important Rule

> **Do not write the story before understanding the activity.**

The story must support the activity.

The activity should not be changed just to fit the story.

---

# 6. CAT and Storification

Karkhana lessons follow:

**Curiosity → Activity → Thinking**

Storification should support this flow.

| CAT | Story Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Curiosity** | Something happens and a question/problem appears |
| **Activity** | Bacchaharu try, make, test, explore, or solve |
| **Thinking** | Bacchaharu notice, compare, discuss, and connect |

A simple way to remember:

> **Story creates the question.**  
> **Activity creates the evidence.**  
> **Thinking creates the meaning.**

---

Use this complete flow:

**Understand CAT**  
↓  
**Find the Core Experience**  
↓  
**Find a Story Situation**  
↓  
**Create OMD**  
↓  
**Build 5 Acts**

↓

↓  
**Write Closure**  
↓

# 8. Find the Core Experience

Before writing the story, ask:

> **What is the most interesting thing bacchaharu will actually experience?**

Examples:

### Measure Mystery 2

Not:

> Volume of irregular objects.

Core experience:

> The water level rises when the stone is placed inside.

### Magnetic Maze

Not:

> Magnetic force.

Core experience:

> The ring moves even when the hand does not touch it.

### Zoom the Zoo

Not:

> Animal communication.

Core experience:

> Animals can give clues through sounds and movements.

The story should grow from the experience.

---

# 9. Find the Story Situation

Turn the lesson idea into a simple child-sized situation.

Examples:

| Lesson Idea | Story Situation |
| --- | --- |
| Magnetism | Move a metal ring without touching it |
| Irregular volume | Find the size of a stone that a ruler cannot measure |
| Fractions | Share something fairly |
| Wind | Move something without touching it |
| Animal communication | Guess an animal without saying its name |
| Patterns | Find what is repeating or changing |
| Time | Fix or complete a clock |
| Filtration | Separate something unwanted from a mixture |

The situation should feel familiar, visual, and easy to imagine.

---

# 10. OMD Framework

OMD helps us build the story engine.

## O — Objective

Ask:

> **What does the character want to do?**

Examples:

- Move the ring through the maze.
- Find the size of a stone.
- Build a bridge.
- Guess the hidden animal.

Avoid:

- Learn about force.
- Understand measurement.
- Know about animals.

---

## M — Motivation

Ask:

> **Why does the character care?**

The character should have a reason.

Examples:

- Wants to finish the maze challenge.
- Wants to make Tara’s gift box.
- Wants to help a friend.
- Wants to win a guessing game.
- Wants to make something work.

---

## D — Difficulty

Ask:

> **What is stopping the character?**

The difficulty creates the curiosity.

Examples:

- The ruler cannot measure the irregular object easily.
- The ring cannot be moved properly with fingers.
- The toy does not move.
- The pieces do not fit.
- The animal name cannot be spoken.

## Important Rule

Do not put the scientific answer inside the Difficulty.

Avoid:

> They need to use magnetic force to move the ring.

Write:

> They need to find another way to move the ring without touching it.

The answer should emerge during the activity.

---

# 11. Simple OMD Flow

**Want**  
↓  
**Reason**  
↓  
**Problem**

Or:

**Character wants something**  
↓  
**There is a reason**  
↓  
**Something stops them**  
↓  
**They need to try**

---

# 12. 5-Act Story Framework

The 5-Act Framework turns the OMD into a complete story.

A simple way to remember:

**Notice → Wonder → Try → Discover → Connect**

---

## ACT 1: Set-Up

Something happens.

Show:

- Who is there?
- Where are they?
- What are they doing?
- What object or situation appears?

Keep the setting simple.

Example:

> छुक्छुके finds a maze game during lunch break.

---

## ACT 2: Escalation

The problem or curiosity begins.

Ask:

- What is difficult?
- What is strange?
- What is not working?
- What question appears?

Example:

> His fingers keep pushing the ring outside the narrow path.

Now he wonders:

> “How can I move it without touching it?”

---

## ACT 3: Turning Point

The characters begin trying.

This is where making, testing, exploring, or experimenting begins.

Characters may:

- Build
- Test
- Move
- Compare
- Rearrange
- Guess
- Try another way

Avoid giving the answer through explanation.

---

## ACT 4: Discovery

Characters notice what happened.

They may say:

- “ओहो!”
- “वाह!”
- “Look!”
- “It changed!”
- “That worked!”
- “Why did that happen?”

The discovery should come from their experience.

---

## ACT 5: Resolution / Connection

Return to the original story problem.

The characters:

- Solve it.
- Notice what they discovered.
- Connect it to another situation.
- End with a small curiosity.

The closure should complete the story loop.

---

# 13. Emotional Flow of the 5 Acts

The story should also feel like this:

### Act 1

**Interesting!**

### Act 2

**Hmm... something is wrong.**

### Act 3

**Let’s try!**

### Act 4

**ओहो! Something happened!**

### Act 5

**Now it makes sense!**

---

# 14. Karkhana Characters and Personality

Characters should have recognizable personalities.

They are not strict roles, but their behavior should remain consistent.

---

## छुक्छुके — Curious Explorer

Typical traits:

- Curious
- Playful
- Easily surprised
- Makes funny mistakes
- Notices unusual things
- Wants to try

Typical lines:

- “ओहो! What is that?”
- “Why did that happen?”
- “Can I try?”
- “लौ! It didn’t work!”

Best used for:

- Starting curiosity
- Making a playful mistake
- Reacting with surprise

Avoid making छुक्छुके know every answer.

---

## तारा — Observant Thinker

Typical traits:

- Calm
- Observant
- Notices details
- Compares things
- Thinks carefully

Typical lines:

- “Wait. Look at this.”
- “These are different.”
- “What if we try it this way?”

Best used for:

- Observing patterns
- Comparing
- Noticing differences

Avoid making her a teacher who explains everything.

---

## जिज्ञासा — Questioner and Wonderer

Typical traits:

- Asks questions
- Curious
- Imaginative
- Thinks of possibilities

Typical lines:

- “But why?”
- “What would happen if...?”
- “Can it work another way?”
- “What else can we try?”

Best used for:

- Deepening curiosity
- Asking “what if?”
- Opening another possibility

Avoid making every line a question.

---

## बोधि — Maker and Experimenter

Typical traits:

- Hands-on
- Creative
- Tries things
- Comfortable with mistakes
- Likes building and testing

Typical lines:

- “I have an idea!”
- “Let’s try.”
- “Let’s change this.”
- “Try again!”

Best used for:

- Moving from question to activity
- Making
- Testing
- Iterating

Avoid making him magically know the correct solution.

---

## जीवन — Thoughtful Connector

Typical traits:

- Reflective
- Calm
- Makes real-life connections
- Listens
- Thinks about what happened

Typical lines:

- “I have seen this before.”
- “This reminds me of...”
- “Where else can we find this?”

Best used for:

- Thinking
- Closure
- Connecting to real life

Avoid giving him long textbook explanations.

---

# 15. Character Interaction

A good group interaction may look like:

**छुक्छुके notices**  
↓  
**जिज्ञासा wonders**  
↓  
**बोधि tries**  
↓  
**तारा observes**  
↓  
**जीवन connects**

But do not use this exact pattern every time.

Characters can overlap.

The important thing is that they behave like children.

---

# 16. Child-Centered Story Rule

Bacchaharu should be the problem solvers.

Avoid:

> Teacher explains the concept.

Prefer:

> Bacchaharu notice something, ask, try, test, and discover.

Avoid:

> “जीवन learned that magnets attract metal.”

Prefer:

> जीवन moved the magnet closer.

> The ring moved.

> “ओहो! It followed!”

---

# 17. Strong Beginning Hook

The story should begin with:

- A situation
- A small problem
- A mystery
- A surprise
- Something not working
- Something unusual

Avoid:

> Today we will learn about wind.

Write:

> The box needs to move, but nobody can touch it.

Avoid:

> Today we will study volume.

Write:

> The ruler worked for the box, but not for the stone.

---

# 18. Clear Story Flow

Every story should follow:

**Situation**  
↓  
**Something unusual happens**  
↓  
**Problem appears**  
↓  
**Character wonders**  
↓  
**They try**  
↓  
**They notice**  
↓  
**They solve/connect**

There should be no sudden jumps.

Ask after every sentence:

> **Does this naturally connect to what happened before?**

If not, rewrite or remove it.

---

# 19. One Idea at a Time

Avoid adding too many problems into one story.

Prefer:

**One event**  
↓  
**One main curiosity**  
↓  
**One problem**  
↓  
**One exploration**

Keep the story focused.

---

# 20. One Place or One Event

For most stories, keep the situation simple.

Examples:

- One classroom moment
- One lunch break
- One playground event
- One home situation
- One festival moment

Avoid unnecessary:

- Time jumps
- Location changes
- Multiple unrelated events

---

# 21. Language Style

Stories should feel like children’s storybooks.

Use:

- Short sentences
- Simple words
- Easy conversations
- Visual actions
- Child-like expressions

Use:

- “ओहो!”
- “वाह!”
- “लौ!”
- “स्याबास!”

Avoid:

- Textbook language
- Long explanations
- Difficult vocabulary
- Over-explaining concepts

---

# 22. Grade-Level Language

## Grade 1–2

- Very short sentences
- Familiar vocabulary
- One idea at a time
- Mostly visible actions
- Very simple questions

## Grade 3–4

- Short conversational sentences
- Simple problems
- Prediction and observation
- Basic reasoning questions

## Grade 5–7

- Slightly deeper challenges
- More comparison
- More reasoning
- More “why” and “what if”
- Still child-friendly and conversational

---

# 23. Dialogue Rules

Dialogue should move the story.

Avoid filler dialogue.

Weak:

> “Wow!”  
> “Yes, wow!”  
> “This is amazing!”

Better:

> “ओहो! The ruler keeps slipping,” said छुक्छुके.

> जिज्ञासा looked at the stone.

> “Then how else can we measure it?”

Each dialogue should do at least one job:

- Show reaction
- Create curiosity
- Show difficulty
- Suggest an idea
- Share an observation
- Connect a discovery

---

# 24. Questions Should Come From the Story

Do not suddenly stop the story to ask an unrelated question.

Use:

**Story event**  
↓  
**Character doubt**  
↓  
**Question**

Example:

> The stone has no straight sides.

Then ask:

> **Why might it be difficult to measure with a ruler?**

This feels natural.

---

# 25. Question Style

Prefer questions that support thinking.

Instead of:

> What is the function of roots?

Try:

> “Why do you think this tree stays firm even when the wind blows?”

Instead of:

> What is magnetic force?

Try:

> “How can the ring move when no hand is touching it?”

Questions should invite observation and reasoning.

---

# 26. MCQ Rules

MCQs should:

- Match the grade.
- Connect to the story.
- Prepare bacchaharu for the activity.
- Include realistic options.
- Avoid silly distractors.
- Avoid making the answer too obvious.

Example:

### Why is the stone difficult to measure with a ruler?

A. It does not have clear straight sides.  
B. It changes shape every second.  
C. It has no size.

Correct: **A**

The wrong options should still make sense within the story context.

---

# 27. Storified Curiosity

Curiosity should create a reason to explore.

A good curiosity should:

1. Start from the story situation.
2. Create one problem.
3. Ask a question.
4. Stop before the answer is fully explained.
5. Lead to:

> **“Let’s try!”**

---

# 28. Do Not Give the Answer Too Early

This is one of the most important rules.

Avoid:

> The magnet attracts the metal ring through paper.

Then:

> Let’s test it.

The activity has already lost its mystery.

Better:

> “My hand is not touching the ring, but it moved!”

> “How did that happen?”

> **Let’s try!**

The activity should create the evidence.

---

# 29. Activity Transition

The activity should feel like the natural next action.

Avoid:

> Now do Activity 1.

Prefer:

> “Can we test this idea?”

> **Let’s try!**

The transition should make the activity feel like the solution to the story problem.

---

# 30. Story and Activity Relationship

A strong story follows:

**Story = Question**  
↓  
**Activity = Test**  
↓  
**Thinking = Meaning**  
↓  
**Closure = Resolution**

Example:

### Story

Can water help find the stone’s size?

### Activity

Measure water before and after adding the stone.

### Thinking

Why did the water level rise?

### Closure

The water level change helped us find the stone’s volume.

---

# 31. Thinking Questions

Thinking questions should help bacchaharu notice what they experienced.

Use questions like:

- What changed?
- What stayed the same?
- What did you notice?
- Why do you think that happened?
- What happened when you changed ___?
- What pattern did you see?
- What worked?
- What did not work?
- What else could you try?
- Where else have you seen this?

Avoid using Thinking only as recall.

---

# 32. Closure

The closure should return to the beginning problem.

Use this pattern:

**Beginning Question**  
↕  
**Activity Experience**  
↕  
**Closure**

Example:

Beginning:

> “How can we measure this stone?”

Closure:

> “We could not measure its sides, but the water helped us find its volume!”

The story loop is now complete.

---

# 33. Closure Rules

A good closure should:

- Answer the original story question.
- Resolve the main difficulty.
- Include a small discovery.
- Connect naturally to what bacchaharu did.
- Stay short.
- Avoid long explanations.
- Sometimes leave one small new curiosity.

Good:

> “वाह! We moved the ring without touching it!”

Then:

> “What else could a magnet move?”

---

# 34. Real-Life Connection

Use familiar situations.

Examples:

- Classroom
- Lunch break
- Garden
- Home
- Festivals
- Toys
- Food
- Friends
- Playground
- Nature
- Everyday objects

Do not force a real-life example if it makes the story unnatural.

---

# 35. Visual and Scene Rules

The story should be easy to illustrate.

Ask:

> **Can this sentence be shown in a picture?**

Prefer visible actions:

- Holding
- Dropping
- Building
- Pouring
- Looking
- Moving
- Pointing
- Testing

Avoid long internal explanations.

---

# 36. Picture-Friendly Writing

Instead of:

> छुक्छुके became confused because he could not understand why the object was not moving correctly.

Write:

> The ring slipped outside the maze.

> छुक्छुके scratched his head.

> “लौ! Why does this keep happening?”

The picture can show the rest.

---

# 37. Common Types of Story Changes

While reviewing stories, these are the main changes to check.

| Type of Change | What to Check |
| --- | --- |
| **Story Flow Change** | Does the story move smoothly? |
| **Curiosity Change** | Is there a meaningful problem or mystery? |
| **Question Change** | Do questions come naturally from characters? |
| **MCQ Change** | Are the options sensible and grade-appropriate? |
| **Language Change** | Are words and sentences simple enough? |
| **Character Dialogue Change** | Are characters driving the story? |
| **Cohesiveness Change** | Are there sudden jumps? |
| **Grade-Level Change** | Is the difficulty suitable for the grade? |
| **OMD Change** | Are Objective, Motivation, Difficulty clear? |
| **5-Act Change** | Does the story build properly? |
| **Closure Change** | Does the ending return to the beginning? |
| **Real-Life Connection Change** | Is the situation relatable? |
| **Scientific Concept Change** | Is the answer being revealed too early? |
| **Activity Transition Change** | Does the story naturally lead to the activity? |
| **Length Change** | Is the story too long or too short? |
| **Tone Change** | Does it feel playful and curious? |
| **Visual/Scene Change** | Can the story be illustrated clearly? |
| **Vocabulary Change** | Are difficult words simplified? |
| **Answer Alignment Change** | Do questions support the learning objective? |
| **Learning Closure Change** | Does the ending help bacchaharu notice what they discovered? |

---

# 38. Most Important Improvement Checks

The most common changes to focus on are:

1. **Make the story cohesive.**
2. **Start curiosity from a real problem.**
3. **Make characters ask natural questions.**
4. **Keep MCQ options realistic.**
5. **Match language to the grade.**
6. **Connect closure back to the beginning.**
7. **Do not reveal the scientific answer too early.**
8. **Make the activity feel like the solution.**
9. **Let children solve, not teachers explain.**
10. **Keep the story visual and easy to follow.**

---

# 39. Story Review Checklist

Before finalizing, check:

## Lesson Check

- [ ] I understand the CAT flow.
- [ ] I understand what bacchaharu actually do.
- [ ] The story matches the activity.
- [ ] The story does not introduce an unrelated idea.

## OMD Check

- [ ] The Objective is something the character wants to do.
- [ ] The Motivation gives a real reason.
- [ ] The Difficulty creates a genuine problem.
- [ ] The Difficulty does not reveal the answer.

## Story Flow Check

- [ ] Act 1 creates a clear situation.
- [ ] Act 2 creates curiosity or difficulty.
- [ ] Act 3 leads into trying/testing.
- [ ] Act 4 includes discovery.
- [ ] Act 5 resolves the original problem.
- [ ] Every sentence connects naturally.

## Character Check

- [ ] Characters sound like children.
- [ ] Characters behave according to their personalities.
- [ ] Children are active problem solvers.
- [ ] One character is not explaining everything.
- [ ] Dialogue is short and useful.

## Curiosity Check

- [ ] The beginning has a hook.
- [ ] There is one clear mystery or problem.
- [ ] Questions come from the story.
- [ ] The answer is not given too early.
- [ ] Bacchaharu should want to test the idea.

## Question Check

- [ ] Questions are connected to what just happened.
- [ ] MCQ options are realistic.
- [ ] Questions match the grade level.
- [ ] Questions prepare bacchaharu for the activity.

## Activity Check

- [ ] The transition feels natural.
- [ ] The activity feels like the next thing the characters would do.
- [ ] The activity creates the evidence or discovery.

## Thinking Check

- [ ] Questions ask bacchaharu to notice and compare.
- [ ] Bacchaharu think from their experience.
- [ ] Questions are not only recall-based.

## Closure Check

- [ ] The original story problem is resolved.
- [ ] The discovery is short and clear.
- [ ] The closure connects to the activity.
- [ ] The ending does not become a textbook explanation.
- [ ] A small new curiosity may remain.

## Grade-Level Check

- [ ] Vocabulary matches the grade.
- [ ] Sentence length matches the grade.
- [ ] The reasoning level is appropriate.
- [ ] The story is easy to follow.

## Picture Check

- [ ] Important moments can be illustrated.
- [ ] Actions are visible.
- [ ] Feelings can be shown visually.
- [ ] Unnecessary description is removed.

---

# 40. Storification Quality Gate

A story is ready only when:

> **The story creates a reason to explore.**

> **The activity helps bacchaharu investigate the story problem.**

> **The thinking helps them make meaning from what happened.**

> **The closure returns to the original problem.**

And finally ask:

> **After reading this story, will bacchaharu think:**  
> **“I want to try this!”**  
> or  
> **“I have to study this”?**

A strong Karkhana story should always move bacchaharu toward:

> **“I want to try!”**

---

When working fast, use this:

### Step 1

What are bacchaharu actually doing?

### Step 2

What interesting experience happens?

### Step 3

What situation can create a reason for it?

### Step 4

Write OMD:

**Want → Reason → Problem**

### Step 5

Write 5 Acts:

**Notice → Wonder → Try → Discover → Connect**

### Step 6

Write Curiosity.

### Step 7

Stop before the answer.

### Step 8

Let the activity create the discovery.

### Step 9

Write the closure.

### Step 10

Check:

> **Does the activity solve the story problem?**

---

# 42. Final Storification Flow

**Understand the Lesson**  
↓  
**Find the Core Experience**  
↓  
**Create a Real-Life Situation**  
↓  
**OMD: Want → Reason → Problem**  
↓  
**5 Acts: Notice → Wonder → Try → Discover → Connect**  
↓  
**Storified Curiosity**  
↓  
**“Let’s try!”**  
↓  
**Hands-on Activity**  
↓  
**Observe and Think**  
↓  
**Resolve the Original Problem**  
↓  
**Short Closure**

---

# 43. Final Principle

The final story should feel like:

> **Children found a mystery → became curious → tried something → experimented → noticed what happened → made a discovery.**

Not:

> **Teacher introduced a topic → teacher explained it → students performed an activity.**
