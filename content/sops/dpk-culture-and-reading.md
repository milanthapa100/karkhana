## Description

This SOP is a reading and practice document for the DPK Unit. It explains the thinking culture that unit members should understand and consciously practice while creating, improving, reviewing, and finalizing lessons.

The purpose is not only to define words. The purpose is to create a shared way of working. Also this is our internal lingo too.

This document should help every DPK member understand:

- Why we treat lessons as a process, not only an output
- How we move from idea to usable lesson/product
- How we test, improve, and learn from real work
- How our culture supports quality lessons

## When This SOP Is Used

Use this SOP when:

- A new DPK member is being onboarded
- A person starts working on lesson creation, lesson improvement, material work, design, video, DOK, or review
- A team member is confused about how DPK thinks and works
- A lesson process feels stuck, slow, or unclear
- The unit is reviewing its working culture
- A checklist or SOP needs to reflect DPK culture

## Core Belief

DPK creates quality lessons through a strong process.

A quality lesson is not created in one perfect attempt. It is created through thinking, making, testing, feedback, and improvement.

Therefore:

- We value process as much as the final output
- We complete small versions quickly
- We test ideas using available resources
- We use feedback as data
- We improve through iteration
- We document what we learn

## Roles and Responsibilities

| Role | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| Unit Curator | Owns this culture document and ensures it is discussed with the unit |
| Unit Member | Reads, understands, and practices the culture while doing work |
| Work Owner | Applies the culture to the task, checklist, SOP, or lesson they own |
| Reviewer | Gives feedback using the culture principles, not only personal preference |
| Peer | Helps test, question, and improve ideas through discussion and practice |

## DPK Culture Principles

### 1. How Learning Happens

As a teacher, you must value how learning actually happens.

There are 3 key ideas but the base of them is this: "Learning is a process."

**Idea 1:** Learning happens when a student actively connects a new experience with prior knowledge.

**Idea 2:** Procedural knowledge: First understand the What, and then How.

**Idea 3:** Metacognition improves the learning.

In DPK work, this means a lesson should not only tell information. It should create a learning experience where students observe, question, try, discuss, reflect, and form understanding.

Think of it like this:

A student does not understand plants only because we say "plants need water". They understand it better when they water one plant, forget another, observe the difference, ask why it happened, and connect that experience to the idea.

When creating or reviewing a lesson, ask:

- What will the student experience first?
- What prior knowledge does the student need?
- What misconception may appear?
- What will the student do with their hands and mind?
- What moment helps the student connect old knowledge with new knowledge?
- What reflection helps the student make meaning?

Practice rule: Do not design only for content coverage. Design for student understanding.

### 2. Persona and Perspective

As a designer, you must value the people who will actually use the lesson.

Persona means understanding the user of the lesson. In DPK, the main users include students and teachers. Sometimes the internal team is also a user because they must prepare, package, teach, review, or improve the lesson.

Perspective means seeing the lesson from the position of the person who will use it.

Think of it like this:

A Grade 3 student, a Grade 8 student, a teacher, and a designer will not experience the same activity in the same way. If we only see the activity from our own side, we miss the real classroom experience.

When using persona, ask:

- Who is the student?
- What grade are they in?
- What language level do they have?
- What materials can they handle?
- What might confuse them?
- What will excite them?
- What does the teacher need to run the lesson?
- What classroom constraint may affect the activity?

Practice rule: Do not design only from the creator's perspective. Design from the learner's and teacher's perspective.

### 3. Material, Cost, and Classroom Experience

As a designer, you must value materials as part of the learning experience, not just as objects.

In DPK, material is not only an object. Material affects cost, packaging, classroom setup, safety, student experience, teacher ease, and lesson quality.

Material = Cost = Classroom Experience

This means every material decision affects the whole lesson system.

Think of it like this:

If an activity needs a rare material, the idea may look good on paper but fail in the classroom. If a simple straw, rubber band, or paper piece can create the same learning, that may be the better design.

When choosing materials, ask:

- Is the material available?
- Is it affordable?
- Is it safe?
- Can it be packed and transported?
- Can students use it easily?
- Can the teacher manage it in class?
- Does it improve the learning experience?
- Is there a simpler material that works better?

Practice rule: A clever activity that cannot work in the classroom is not yet a complete lesson.

### 4. Scientific Thinking

As a designer, you must value science as a way of thinking, not only as content.

Scientific thinking means using observation, questions, hypotheses, experiments, analysis, and conclusions to create reliable learning experiences.

In DPK, scientific thinking protects lessons from becoming only interesting ideas. It makes sure activities are testable, explainable, and repeatable.

Use this pattern:

1. Observe something
2. Ask a question
3. Make a hypothesis
4. Create an experiment or activity
5. Observe what happens
6. Analyze the result
7. Conclude what can be learned

When reviewing a lesson, ask:

- What observation starts the learning?
- What question drives the activity?
- What can students predict?
- What experiment or action tests the idea?
- What evidence will students see?
- What conclusion should students reach?
- Can the activity be repeated with many students?

Practice rule: Without a question, the activity becomes weak. Without evidence, the conclusion becomes weak.

### 5. Research

As a researcher, you must value knowledge before judgment.

Research means gathering knowledge before and during creation. It includes online research, reading, observing, testing, talking to people, looking at existing lessons, and physically experiencing the problem.

In DPK, research is not only searching the internet. Research can be:

- Reading curriculum
- Checking old lessons
- Observing students
- Asking teachers
- Exploring materials
- Testing activity ideas
- Looking at real-life examples
- Comparing multiple sources

Think of it like this:

If you are designing an activity about crutches, reading online is not enough. Watch someone use crutches, try walking with restricted movement, talk to a user, and then design from what you learned.

When researching, ask:

- What do I already know?
- What do I need to know?
- What does the curriculum require?
- What have students or teachers already said?
- What examples already exist?
- What material or classroom constraint should I understand?
- What should I physically try before deciding?

Practice rule: Research should help make the work more real, not just more complicated.

### 6. Design Thinking

As a designer, you must value solving the right problem before making the solution.

Design thinking is a way to solve problems by understanding users, defining the problem, creating ideas, prototyping, testing, and improving.

For DPK, design thinking helps us create lessons that are useful, usable, and meaningful for students and teachers.

Use this simple flow:

1. Empathize — Understand the learner, teacher, context, and constraints
2. Define — Clarify the problem or learning need
3. Ideate — Create possible lesson or activity ideas
4. Prototype — Build a simple version
5. Test — Try it, review it, and collect feedback
6. Improve — Make changes based on evidence

When using design thinking, ask:

- Whose problem are we solving?
- What is the real learning need?
- What are different possible ways to solve it?
- What is the smallest version we can test?
- What did we learn from testing?
- What should change next?

Practice rule: Design thinking is not a poster. It is a working habit.

### 7. 0 to 1

As a designer, you must value completing the first version quickly.

0 to 1 means moving from nothing to something.

In DPK, this means completing the first usable version of an idea quickly instead of waiting for the perfect version. A first version gives the team something to see, discuss, test, and improve.

Think of it like this:

If you are cooking rice for the first time, you can spend one hour asking and researching, or you can cook a small batch in fifteen minutes and learn from it. The first version gives you something real to improve.

Use 0 to 1 when:

- The task feels unclear
- The idea is still in your head
- The team is discussing too much without making
- You need feedback
- A lesson, prototype, checklist, or document does not exist yet

Practice rule: Do not stay at zero. Create the first version, even if it is rough.

### 8. Skateboard

As a designer, you must value the smallest usable version.

Skateboard means creating the smallest usable version that can move.

The idea is: before building a full vehicle, make a skateboard. It may not be the final form, but it lets someone use it, test it, and give feedback.

In DPK, a skateboard can be:

- A rough activity flow
- A paper prototype
- A draft CAT
- A sample material setup
- A one-page lesson idea
- A simple checklist
- A quick video sample

When making a skateboard, ask:

- What is the smallest version that still shows the idea?
- Can someone try it?
- Can we learn from it?
- What can be left out for now?
- What must be included for testing?

Practice rule: A small usable version is better than a large unfinished idea.

### 9. Prototyping

As a designer, you must value learning through making.

Prototyping means creating a basic working model of the idea using available resources.

In DPK, prototyping helps us test whether an activity, material, lesson flow, explanation, or classroom setup can work before we invest too much time.

A prototype does not need to be beautiful. It needs to help the team learn.

Prototype examples:

- Use paper instead of final printed material
- Use rough sketches instead of final design
- Use available objects instead of final materials
- Act out a classroom flow before writing the final lesson
- Test one activity step before building the whole lesson

When prototyping, ask:

- What question am I testing?
- What can I build quickly?
- What resource can I use right now?
- Who can try this?
- What result will tell me whether to continue, change, or stop?

Practice rule: Prototype to learn, not to impress.

### 10. TMPI

As a designer, you must value the cycle of thinking, making, playing, and improving.

TMPI is Karkhana's working cycle: Think, Make, Play, Improve.

Use TMPI as a practical rhythm for DPK work.

**Think:**

- Understand the task
- Clarify the learning goal
- Identify constraints
- Decide what to try

**Make:**

- Create the first version
- Build the prototype
- Draft the checklist, lesson, material, design, or flow

**Play:**

- Try it
- Test it
- Use it like a student, teacher, or reviewer would
- Notice what happens

**Improve:**

- Reflect on what worked and what did not
- Use feedback
- Update the work
- Record the change

Practice rule: Do not only think. Do not only make. Complete the cycle.

### 11. Culture of Experimentation

As a designer, you must value trying instead of waiting for perfect certainty.

Culture of experimentation means we solve problems by trying, testing, learning, and improving.

In DPK, experimentation means we do not wait for perfect certainty before acting. We observe, research, prototype, test, and learn from the result.

Experimentation is useful when:

- The team is unsure which idea will work
- There are multiple possible solutions
- The lesson depends on student behavior
- A material may or may not work
- A classroom flow feels risky
- Feedback is unclear

Before experimenting, define:

- What are we testing?
- Why are we testing it?
- What do we expect will happen?
- Who or what will we test with?
- What evidence will we collect?
- What decision will we make after testing?

Practice rule: An experiment must create learning. If nothing is recorded, the experiment is incomplete.

### 12. Feedback

As a designer, you must value feedback as data for improvement.

Feedback is information that helps improve the work.

In DPK, feedback can come from students, teachers, unit members, reviewers, testing, classroom observation, material use, or self-review.

Feedback should be treated as data, not as personal attack or final approval.

When collecting feedback, ask:

- What worked?
- What did not work?
- What confused the learner or teacher?
- What should change?
- Is the issue about learning, usability, feasibility, language, material, cost, or design?
- What decision will we make from this feedback?

Feedback categories:

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Learning | Affects student understanding or learning objective |
| Usability | Affects how easy the lesson is to use |
| Feasibility | Affects time, cost, material, or classroom practicality |
| Clarity | Affects language, instruction, flow, or explanation |
| Experience | Affects engagement, curiosity, or classroom energy |

Practice rule: Do not collect feedback and leave it unused. Decide, update, and document.

### 13. Iteration

As a designer, you must value consistent improvement over one-time perfection.

Iteration means opening and closing a piece of work within a limited time so that it improves through repeated effort.

Opening means finding, questioning, testing, and making changes. Closing means documenting the change, checking quality, and preparing the work for the next step.

In DPK, iteration is not working endlessly on one thing. It is consistent improvement over time.

An iteration should include:

- What was opened?
- What was changed?
- Why was it changed?
- What feedback or evidence caused the change?
- What output was produced?
- What still needs attention?
- Where was the update recorded?

Practice rule: If the change is not recorded, the iteration is not complete.

## Steps

Use this cycle when applying the culture to any task:

1. Understand the learner, teacher, and context
2. Research the topic, material, and existing work
3. Define the learning or work problem
4. Move from 0 to 1 by creating a first version
5. Build a skateboard version that can be tested
6. Prototype using available resources
7. Test through TMPI
8. Collect feedback
9. Improve through iteration
10. Record the decision, change, and output

## Culture Checklist

Before finalizing any DPK work, check:

- The learner or user is clearly understood
- The teacher or classroom perspective has been considered
- The material, cost, and classroom experience have been checked
- The learning objective is clear
- Research has been done beyond guessing
- Scientific thinking is visible in the activity or process
- A first version was created instead of waiting for perfection
- A small usable version was tested
- A prototype was used to learn something
- TMPI was completed at least once
- Feedback was collected and categorized
- Changes were made based on evidence
- The iteration was documented

## Outputs

After using this SOP, the reader should be able to:

- Explain the core DPK culture principles
- Apply the principles to a real task
- Use the culture while creating or reviewing lessons
- Give feedback using shared language
- Decide how to move stuck work forward
- Record improvements clearly

## Common Mistakes

- Treating culture words as theory only
- Waiting for the perfect version before making anything
- Doing research only online
- Creating activities without student or teacher perspective
- Choosing materials without checking cost or classroom use
- Prototyping only after the final design is almost complete
- Collecting feedback but not deciding actions
- Iterating without recording what changed
- Treating the final lesson as an outcome instead of a process

## Quality Gate

This culture SOP has been practiced properly when a unit member can take a real DPK task and explain:

- Who the work is for
- What learning or work problem is being solved
- What research informed the work
- What first version was created
- What prototype or skateboard version was tested
- What feedback was received
- What changed after feedback
- Where the change was recorded
