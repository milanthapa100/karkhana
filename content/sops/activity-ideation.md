## Description

This SOP explains how a DPK team member comes up with activity ideas, filters them against the learning objective and classroom reality, and settles on the best one to take forward into flow design and a Curiosity Activity and Thinking (CAT).

Ideation is the thinking stage. The goal is to produce several candidate ideas, not a perfect final lesson. A strong activity is one that creates understanding, not just interest.

## When This SOP Is Used

Use this SOP when:

- You are starting a new lesson and need activity ideas
- An existing activity feels weak, boring, or ineffective
- Multiple ideas exist and you need to choose between them
- A lesson needs a fresh approach for the same objective
- You want to make sure the activity actually creates learning, not just engagement

## Inputs Needed Before Starting

- The learning objective for the lesson
- The grade, subject, and topic
- The students and their language level
- Classroom constraints (time, space, number of students, furniture)
- Available material and budget
- Curriculum requirements if any

## Steps

### Step 1: Restate the Learning Objective

Write the objective in your own words. Make sure it states the understanding or skill the student should gain, not just the topic.

Weak: "Student learns about plants."
Strong: "Student can explain why plants need water by testing what happens when water is missing."

A restated objective tells you what the activity must achieve.

### Step 2: List Candidate Activities

Generate as many activity ideas as possible without judging them yet. Draw from:

- Hands-on experiments and building tasks
- Demonstrations the teacher runs
- Games and challenges
- Role play and scenarios
- Stories and simulations
- Discussion and questioning
- Observation of real examples

For each idea, write a one-line description of what the student would do.

### Step 3: Map Each Idea to the Objective

For every idea, ask: does the student actually experience or practice the learning, or do they just watch?

Remove or set aside any idea that does not clearly connect to the objective. A fun idea that teaches nothing is a distraction, not an activity.

### Step 4: Filter by Classroom Reality

Check each surviving idea against the real constraints:

- Can it run in the available time?
- Can students do it with the available materials?
- Does it fit the space and class size?
- Is it safe?
- Can the teacher manage it alone?
- Is the material affordable and packable?

Mark each idea as Workable, Risky, or Not Workable.

### Step 5: Check the Learning Experience

For the workable ideas, check whether it creates real understanding:

- What will the student observe, try, question, or build?
- What prior knowledge does it connect to?
- What misconception might it clear up?
- What does the student make meaning from?

### Step 6: Pick the Best Idea

Choose the idea that best balances:

- Strong connection to the objective
- Low cost and classroom risk
- Real student engagement and understanding
- Simplicity of material and setup

Keep the other candidates noted. They may become alternates or future lessons.

### Step 7: Record the Decision

Write down:

- The chosen activity and why
- The objective it serves
- The material and setup it needs
- The learning experience it creates
- The alternates that were set aside

Pass this on to flow ideation. Record the decision in the work log or tracker.

## Activity Ideation Checklist

Before moving on to flow design, check:

- The learning objective is written as a student outcome
- At least 3 candidate ideas were generated
- Each idea was mapped to the objective
- Unsuitable ideas were removed or set aside
- Workable, risky, and not workable ideas were sorted
- The chosen idea connects to the objective
- The chosen idea creates real understanding, not just interest
- Material and classroom constraints were checked
- The decision and reasoning were recorded

## Outputs

- A chosen activity idea
- Clear reasoning for the choice
- Alternates noted for later
- A record in the work log or tracker

## Common Mistakes

- Picking an idea for fun before checking the objective
- Choosing an activity that cannot run in the classroom
- Ignoring cost, material, or setup limits
- Creating engagement without understanding
- Settling on the first idea without alternatives
- Moving to flow design before the activity is decided

## Quality Gate

The activity is ready for flow design only when it clearly serves the learning objective and can realistically run in the classroom with the available resources.
