## Description

This SOP explains how a DPK team member designs a Curiosity Activity and Thinking (CAT). A CAT is an activity built to spark a student's curiosity first, then use that curiosity to drive thinking, questioning, and understanding.

A CAT is not a normal practice task or worksheet. It is the moment in the lesson where students become curious enough to ask their own questions and think for themselves. It supports the flow of a lesson just like the "hook" or "experience" part.

## When This SOP Is Used

Use this SOP when:

- You need an opening activity that grabs student curiosity
- A lesson starts flat and students are not interested
- You want students to think and ask questions, not just receive answers
- A concept is abstract and needs a concrete, curious introduction
- A lesson needs a moment where students observe, wonder, and investigate

## Inputs Needed Before Starting

- The learning objective
- The flow position where the CAT will sit
- The grade, subject, and topic
- Students and their language level
- Classroom constraints (time, space, group size)
- Materials that can realistically be used

## Core Principle

Curiosity comes from a gap. A student becomes curious when they notice something they cannot yet explain: a surprise, a contradiction, a mystery, or a challenge.

The CAT must create that gap, then give students room to think about it.

## CAT Design Structure

A good CAT has five parts:

1. The Spark: something students observe or experience that surprises them
2. The Question: what the student naturally wonders next
3. The Thinking: time for students to predict, reason, or hypothesize
4. The Investigation: a small action that tests the student's idea
5. The Share: students explain what they noticed and figured out

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify the Gap in the Topic

Ask: what part of this topic is surprising or hard to explain at first glance? What would a student find unexpected?

The gap is your raw material. If there is no gap, the topic still needs a question or a mystery to build curiosity.

### Step 2: Design the Spark

Pick the smallest, most concrete experience that shows the gap. The spark should be observable and quick. Examples:

- A surprising demonstration
- An unusual object or material
- A picture or video that contradicts expectations
- A tricky challenge or puzzle
- A strange result from a simple test

The spark must not explain the answer. It must raise the question.

### Step 3: Frame the Question

Write the question you want students to wonder about. Keep it in student language. The question guides everything that follows.

Weak: "What are the properties of water?"
Strong: "Why does this paper stay dry inside the cup?"

### Step 4: Build the Thinking Time

Give students a moment to predict and reason before any answer is given. Use:

- Turn and talk with a partner
- Individual written prediction
- Vote or hand raise
- Reasons for their guess

This is where thinking starts. Do not rush to the answer.

### Step 5: Add a Small Investigation

Give students a quick, simple action to test their ideas. It does not need to be a full experiment. It needs to produce evidence they can observe.

Check the investigation is safe, cheap, and fits the classroom.

### Step 6: Plan the Share

Decide how students surface what they saw and figured out. This may be a class discussion, a quick report, or answers to one guiding question.

Connect what they discovered back to the learning objective.

### Step 7: Record the CAT

Write down the spark, question, thinking prompt, investigation, and share. Note the timing and materials for each part. Save it with the lesson and add it to the tracker.

## CAT Quality Checklist

Before using, check:

- The topic has a curiosity gap
- The spark is observable and quick
- The spark does not spoil the answer
- The question is in student language
- Thinking time is included before answers
- The investigation is safe, cheap, and simple
- The share connects back to the objective
- The whole CAT fits its flow position and timing
- Materials are listed and realistic
- The CAT is recorded and tracked

## Outputs

- A designed CAT with spark, question, thinking time, investigation, and share
- A record in the work log or tracker

## Common Mistakes

- Explaining the answer inside the spark
- Making the spark too long
- Skipping the thinking time and asking for answers immediately
- Making the investigation a full complex experiment
- Using a curiosity gap that has nothing to do with the objective
- Forgetting to connect student discoveries back to the lesson

## Quality Gate

The CAT is ready only when a student would become genuinely curious from the spark, think their own idea before the answer, and test it with a small action.
